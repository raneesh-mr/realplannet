The ₹40 lakh decision most people make on a hunch

14 August 2026·8 min read·India

Add it up honestly. Four years of fees. Four years of not earning. Coaching, hostel, materials, the entrance-exam year some people repeat. For a private engineering or management degree in India, the true cost sits comfortably in the tens of lakhs once you count the income you didn't make.

Now ask how the decision got made.

Usually: a parent's view of which fields are respectable, a cousin who did well in something, a coaching centre with an incentive to sell a particular stream, and a general sense that engineering or medicine is safe. Often decided at seventeen, by someone with no work experience, advised by people whose picture of the job market is thirty years out of date.

We apply more rigour to buying a phone than to the single largest investment most families ever make.

Why the usual advice fails

It isn't that the people advising you are wrong-headed. It's that the questions being asked can't produce a usable answer.

"What are you passionate about?"

A seventeen-year-old has been exposed to perhaps a dozen occupations, mostly through family and television. Passion requires familiarity. Asking someone to identify a calling from a sample of twelve is asking them to guess and then feel guilty when the guess doesn't hold.

Interest also isn't fixed. It grows where you develop competence. Plenty of people who love their work grew into it rather than arriving pre-loving it.

"What's in demand?"

Better, but it has a timing flaw. Demand is reported on a lag, degrees take three to five years, and everyone reads the same article. By the time a cohort graduates into the "hot" field, the shortage that made it hot has been over-corrected by the people who read the same advice. Chasing current demand reliably lands you in a surplus.

"Take an aptitude test"

Most free tests measure preference, not aptitude, and return a list of job titles with no reasoning attached. "You should consider: architect, graphic designer, urban planner." Based on what? Weighted how? Against which alternatives? A recommendation you can't interrogate is a horoscope with better formatting.

The four things that actually decide it

A usable framework looks at four things together. Any one alone gives a bad answer.

1. Aptitude — what you're actually good at

Not grades. Grades measure how well you performed against a syllabus with a specific teacher. Aptitude is the underlying pattern: spatial reasoning, verbal reasoning, numerical fluency, pattern recognition, working memory. These are stable, measurable, and they predict which work will feel like effort and which will feel like grinding.

2. Working temperament — what you can sustain

This one is chronically ignored and it ends more careers than aptitude ever does.

Can you tolerate ambiguity, or do you need defined problems? Do you recover energy alone or around people? Do you want visible output at the end of each day, or can you work eighteen months toward one outcome? Do you need autonomy, or is structure a relief?

Someone with excellent quantitative aptitude and a low tolerance for isolated deep work will be technically capable and quietly miserable in research. The aptitude was never the problem.

3. Market reality — where the work actually is

Not "what's hot". Structural questions. Is the field growing or consolidating? Is entry gated by credential, portfolio or network? What does the salary curve look like at year one, year five, year ten — because some fields start low and compound steeply, and others do the reverse. How exposed is it to automation over your working life, and which parts?

4. Life constraints — what you're actually optimising for

Do you need to earn early because family depends on it? Is relocation possible, or must this work from a specific city? Is there a family business in the picture? Do you want work that travels internationally, or one that stays put?

These aren't compromises on the "real" decision. They are the decision. A recommendation that ignores them is advice for a person who doesn't exist.

Where the four intersect

The point isn't to score four separate lists and pick the highest total. It's to find where they overlap — and, just as usefully, to see clearly where they don't.

PatternWhat it means
Strong aptitude, wrong temperamentYou'll be competent and drained. Common in medicine and law. Look for adjacent roles using the same aptitude differently.
Right temperament, weak marketSustainable but financially tight. Fine if constraints allow. Painful if they don't.
Strong market, no aptitudeThe classic trap. You'll compete against people for whom it's easier, and you'll lose slowly.
Everything aligns except constraintsThe most fixable one. Often a sequencing problem, not a direction problem — right field, wrong order.

That last row matters more than people expect. A lot of "wrong choice" outcomes are actually right choices attempted in the wrong sequence — a degree that needed two years of earning first, or a field that needed a different entry point.

What good output looks like

A useful recommendation is not a job title. It's a job title plus the reasoning, plus what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

Compare these two:

  • "You should consider data analytics."
  • "Your numerical reasoning is in the top band and your tolerance for ambiguous, undefined problems is low. Data analytics fits both — the problems arrive scoped. Data science, which people often suggest alongside it, does not: the work is far more open-ended and you'd likely find it draining. Entry is portfolio-gated rather than degree-gated, so a three-year degree plus demonstrable projects beats a five-year integrated programme on both cost and time."

The second one you can argue with. That's exactly what makes it useful. You can take it to a parent, a teacher or someone working in the field and stress-test it. The first is a horoscope.

If you can't argue with the recommendation, you can't trust it.

If you're deciding right now

Three things worth doing before you commit, whatever framework you use:

  1. Talk to someone five years in, not twenty. The person twenty years in entered a different market and mostly remembers the good parts. Five years in is close enough to remember the entry reality and far enough to have perspective.
  2. Ask what the work is on a Tuesday. Not the outcomes, not the title. What do you actually do between 10am and 4pm on an ordinary day? Most disappointment lives in the gap between the title and the Tuesday.
  3. Write down what would make this the wrong choice. If you can't name a scenario, you haven't examined the decision — you've justified one you'd already made.

The honest bottom line

No framework removes the uncertainty. You are forecasting a market a decade out and a version of yourself you haven't met yet. Anyone selling certainty is selling something.

What a framework does is change the failure mode. Decide on a hunch and you may end up somewhere wrong with no idea which assumption broke. Decide on stated reasoning and you can see what changed, and correct in year two instead of year eight.

That's the whole value. Not certainty — correctability.
