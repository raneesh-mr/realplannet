Your service charge went up. Here's how to find out why.

18 August 2026·9 min read·UAE

The invoice arrives with one number on it. A rate, your unit's area, a total, a due date. Nothing that tells you what changed since last year.

So you ask. The reply is that costs have gone up — chiller, insurance, minimum wage at the cleaning contractor, take your pick. It is probably true. It is also unfalsifiable as stated, because at no point in that conversation does anyone show you a document.

Here is the part most owners never find out: the document exists. Your building's rate was approved before it could be billed to you, the approval is published, and the budget behind it is a line-by-line breakdown that you are entitled to see. Almost nobody asks.

You are not arguing about the number. You are asking to see the workings behind it — and those workings are a matter of record.

What is the number actually made of?

A service charge invoice looks like one charge. It is normally three, stacked on top of each other, and they are governed differently.

ComponentWhat it pays forWhy it moves
General fundThis year's running costs — security, cleaning, common-area utilities and chiller, insurance, lift and fire contracts, landscaping, pest control, waste, plus the management fee.Contract renewals, utility tariffs, insurance premiums, scope changes. The volatile part.
Reserve fundReplacements that are years away — lifts, chillers, facade sealant, fire panels, pumps, waterproofing. Sized by a lifecycle study of the actual asset.Reassessment of the study, or catch-up after years of underfunding. Should be stable if it was set properly.
Master community chargeThe district outside your building — shared roads, public landscaping, community security, district infrastructure.Set by the master developer, not by your building's manager. Frequently the line owners complain to the wrong party about.

That third row explains a lot of misdirected frustration. If your total went up and your building's own rate did not, the increase came from a body your management company neither controls nor negotiates with.

Who signs off before it reaches you?

In Dubai, nothing is chargeable to an owner until RERA has approved the budget it comes from. The sequence is fixed.

The management company builds a twelve-month budget covering the general and reserve funds. It submits that budget to RERA through Mollak, the Land Department's platform for jointly owned property funds. Supporting material goes with it — service contracts, utility and insurance documentation, prior-year actuals, and competitive tenders for the major service lines rather than a single quote from an incumbent. RERA reviews it, and only once approved can the charge be levied.

Collection then runs through Mollak into a supervised account. Cash is not an accepted route. The management company signs on that account; it does not own the money in it, and every payment out has to trace to an approved line.

Two things follow from this that are worth holding onto. First, an unexplained increase is not a private decision by your building manager — it was submitted and approved, which means there is paperwork. Second, if the charge you are being asked to pay does not match the approved budget, that is not a disagreement. That is a compliance issue, and RERA is the body it belongs to.

Where can you check your building's approved rate?

Three places, none of which require anyone's permission.

The DLD Service Charge Index

The Dubai Land Department publishes an approved annual rate per square foot for registered buildings, searchable by building name. It is the fastest sanity check available: find your building, note the rate, multiply by your unit's built-up area. That is your expected annual figure, before master community charges.

If the invoice materially exceeds it, you have a specific, documented question rather than a general complaint. If it matches, the honest conclusion is that the charge is what it is, and the argument you actually want to have is about the budget's contents, not its total.

Mollak and the Dubai REST app

As a registered owner you can see your own invoices, payment history and the registered budget for your building. This is where the abstraction ends and the line items begin.

The management company, in writing

For the two documents that explain everything else: the approved budget for the current year, and the prior year's audited accounts showing actual spend against what was budgeted. That second document is the one that matters most, and the one least often requested.

What separates a justified increase from a lazy one

Costs genuinely rise. The question is whether the increase in front of you is evidenced or asserted.

A justified increase looks likeA weak one looks like
Named line items with the change shown against last year's actual spend.A single percentage applied across the whole budget.
Fresh tenders for the contracts that rose, with more than one bidder.The incumbent contractor renewed without competition, at a higher price.
Prior-year underspend identified and carried back into the budget.No reference to last year's actuals at all.
A reserve contribution traceable to a lifecycle study.A reserve figure that moves with no explanation of what changed in the asset.
The management fee stated as its own line.The management fee absorbed into "administration" or not visible.

Two figures are worth knowing the shape of, with a caveat attached to both. Management fees and reserve contributions are each usually quoted as a percentage of the budget, and market commentary puts management fees in the low double digits and reserve contributions in the single digits. Those are observed market ranges, not regulated ceilings — nobody is in breach for sitting outside them. What they give you is a reason to ask a question, not a basis for a claim. Ask what the fee covers and what the reserve percentage is derived from. The quality of the answer tells you more than the number did.

What to actually do

  1. Look up your building on the DLD Service Charge Index — establish the approved rate before you have any conversation about the invoice.
  2. Do the arithmetic yourself — area × rate, then compare against what you were billed, and identify separately what is master community charge.
  3. Request the approved budget and the prior-year audited accounts in writing — email, not a conversation at the desk. You want budget versus actual, not a summary.
  4. Read the biggest three or four lines, not all of them — security, chiller and common-area utilities, insurance and lift contracts usually carry most of the movement. The rest is noise.
  5. Ask what was tendered this year and what was renewed — a renewal at a higher price with no competing bid is the single most common soft spot in a budget.
  6. Raise it with the owners committee first, then RERA — the committee reviews the budget and feeds concerns upward; RERA holds the approval and the authority. Your developer holds neither.
  7. Keep your account current while you query it — arrears typically cost you standing, including eligibility to vote or serve on the committee. Paying under protest preserves the position you are arguing from.

The honest caveat

Knowing all of this will not usually reduce your service charge. Most increases turn out to be real, and the outcome of a well-run enquiry is often that you understand a number you still have to pay. That is a legitimate result, and worth having — but anyone promising you a reduction is selling something.

The mechanics also vary more than a single article can hold. Older buildings sit under transitional arrangements, branded residences carry contractual layers a standard tower does not, and villa communities work differently again. Percentages quoted as market norms are exactly that: commentary, not law.

This is a general explanation of how service charges work in Dubai, not legal or financial advice about your building. Your registered documents and your approved budget are the arbiters. If something here contradicts them, they win.

What the enquiry is actually worth is smaller and more durable than a refund. A building whose owners read the accounts gets budgeted differently from one whose owners never ask. Not because anyone is behaving badly, but because unexamined numbers drift, and examined ones don't.
