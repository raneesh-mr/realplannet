You own the apartment. You don't run the building.

17 August 2026·8 min read·UAE

A lift has been out for three days. You mention it at the front desk. They are polite, they take a note, they say it has been passed on. Passed on to whom, exactly, nobody says.

A week later a service charge invoice arrives. You pay it, because you always do. You could not say with any confidence what it covers, who approved the amount, or who decided that a lift repair was less urgent than whatever else got done that week.

Most owners assume a building works like a company they part-own — you pay in, you vote, the majority decides. In Dubai that stopped being true on 18 November 2019, when Law No. 6 of 2019 came into force and abolished owners associations as legal entities. A surprising number of people who own apartments here still do not know it.

Owners in Dubai do not approve their own building's budget. RERA does.

Who is actually in your building?

In a typical serviced or branded tower there are three organisations you will physically encounter, and one you almost never will — which is the one that makes the decisions that matter most.

Who you meetWhat they ownWho appointed them
The concierge or residences teamHow the building feels. Arrivals, parcels, bookings, requests, amenity access.The hotel owner or brand, in a branded residence. Not the owners.
The management companyWhether the building is legal and funded. The budget, every contract, insurance, compliance, communication.The developer, with RERA approval — or RERA directly.
The maintenance contractorWhether the building works. Plant, MEP, cleaning, waste, and the record of every job.The management company.
RERA (never seen)Whether anything can be charged to you at all.Statutory, as an agency of the Dubai Land Department.

Notice what that table does not contain: you. Owners appoint none of these parties and can remove none of them.

Why nobody in the lobby can authorise anything

Every party in a building was appointed by someone above them, and that is who they answer to. The chain runs from the master developer, to the building's developer, to the management company, to the contractors. Each link contracts only the next one down.

Two consequences follow, and both cause friction daily.

The concierge sits outside the chain entirely

In a branded residence the front-of-house team works for the brand, under a separate agreement. They are excellent at what they are appointed to do. They have no authority over maintenance, no budget, and no ability to instruct a technician. When they say they will pass it on, that is not a brush-off. It is an accurate description of the only thing they are permitted to do.

The owners committee advises, it does not decide

Since the 2019 law, committee members are selected by RERA from resident owners — up to nine of them. They are not elected by the owners, they cannot sign a contract, they cannot commit the owners' money, and they cannot instruct a contractor.

What they can do is genuinely useful: check that the management company is doing its job, review the budget and make recommendations, and escalate owner concerns to RERA. That last one is the lever most people never pull.

Where your service charge actually goes

The money splits into two funds, and almost all owner attention goes to the wrong one.

General fundReserve fund
Everything that keeps the building running this year.Money set aside now for the replacements that come later.
Maintenance, common-area utilities, insurance, security, cleaning, landscaping, lift and fire contracts, management fees, minor improvements.Chillers, lifts, facade sealant, fire panels, pumps, waterproofing — sized by a lifecycle study and built up over years.
Scrutinised at every renewal, because it is the number that changes.Barely discussed, because it buys nothing you can see today.

The cycle is the same everywhere. The management company prepares a twelve-month budget covering both funds. RERA approves it — and until that happens, nothing is legally chargeable to an owner. Each owner's share is then set by the size of their unit, not by how much of anything they personally use. It is invoiced in quarterly instalments through Mollak, the Land Department's collection platform, into a ring-fenced account held in the owners' name. The management company signs on that account. It does not own it.

Every payment out of that account has to trace back to an approved budget line. Which is why "can you just get this done quickly" is a harder request than it sounds.

The clock nobody is watching

This is the expensive part, and it is almost entirely invisible from inside a lobby.

For a period after completion, construction defects are somebody else's financial problem — the developer's, the main contractor's, and their subcontractors'. Structural liability for the people who designed and built the asset runs for ten years from completion under the UAE Civil Code, and a claim has to be brought within three years of the defect being discovered.

Once those windows close, there is nobody left to call. A failed facade, a cracked podium, a chiller that was undersized from day one — all of it becomes a line in your service charge, or a special levy if the reserve fund was set too low in the years when everybody was arguing to keep the charge down.

Which is the real reason the reserve fund deserves more attention than the general fund. The general fund decides what this year costs. The reserve fund decides whether year eleven is routine or a crisis.

What to actually do

  1. Get the building's declaration — the registered document that defines what is yours, what is common, and how costs are apportioned. It governs everything and most owners have never read it.
  2. Ask for the approved budget and the reserve fund study — not the summary. The approved budget and the lifecycle study behind the reserve contribution.
  3. Find out which category your building is registered under — it determines who legally holds the management role, who can remove them, and whether a committee exists at all.
  4. Report faults in writing, into the system — a message to the front desk that never reaches a work order does not exist six months later when the same fault recurs.
  5. Check the defect clock for your building — completion date, warranty position, and whether anyone is actively pursuing outstanding defects before the window shuts.
  6. Escalate to RERA, not to the developer — the developer may be a debtor, a defect counterparty and the appointing party all at once. RERA is the route with actual authority.

The honest caveat

None of this makes a lift arrive faster this week, and knowing the structure is not the same as having influence over it. Building categories differ, older buildings sit under transitional arrangements, and branded residences carry contractual layers that a standard tower does not. Two apartments in the same postcode can be governed quite differently.

This is a general explanation of how jointly owned property works in Dubai, not legal advice about your building. Get your own registered documents and read them. If something in this piece contradicts what your management company is telling you, the documents are the arbiter — not this article, and not the conversation at the front desk.

The point is narrower than it sounds. Most owner frustration comes from asking the right question of the wrong party. Once you know who is appointed by whom, you stop wasting energy on people who were never able to help you, and start spending it where a decision can actually be made.
